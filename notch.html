Glass-fiber-reinforced polyurethane radomes

I replaced brittle 3D-printed radomes with glass-fiber-reinforced polyurethane radomes by designing and 3D-printing the molds in SolidWorks. The new radomes are now on production units and survive 10 ft drops that shattered the previous version.

The previous radomes were 3D printed. We optimized them to be as strong and good looking as possible, but even with specialty filaments and extremely fine print resolution they were severely limited.

The new radomes are cast in 3D-printed molds. The outer halves are hard, made from PLA. The inner shell is soft TPU with a rigid skeleton, so the mold comes apart easily after casting. The soft TPU part also leaves threaded inserts in the radome, which are used to mount the antenna inside.

This type of mold has a vapor-smoothed ASA inner jacket that leaves the cast radome with a smooth finish that needs no post-processing. On previous versions, sanding out the layer lines left by the molds took a lot of time and effort.

Automated paint application with a CNC spray gantry

I automated a proprietary paint application process by building a CNC spray gantry and writing custom G-code to drive the paint gun. The project combined mechanical, electrical, and software design to produce consistent coats for a performance-sensitive application.

The gantry is built from an HVLP spray gun mounted on a commercial laser engraver. Air for the gun comes from a compressor, passes through a solenoid valve and a regulator, and then enters the gun.

Controlling the spray with G-code is the best approach, and that means controlling the air through G-code too. To do that, I used a DMM to test different ports and G-code commands on the board, looking for one that gave a high/low output, which is what a solenoid needs. I found a working port that responds to mist coolant commands, and I built a driver circuit on it to switch the solenoid valve.

Any pattern drawn in Fusion 360 can be turned into G-code, but that G-code has to be processed specifically for the spray gantry. I wrote a Python script that, among other things, wraps the spindle commands in coolant commands so the G-code switches the air on and off and applies the paint correctly.

After I send the G-code with UGS, the gantry goes to work.

Ventilated fume enclosure with active exhaust

I enabled the automated coating process to run safely in-house by designing a ventilated fume enclosure with active exhaust in Fusion 360 and CNC-machining parts of it.

The enclosure frame is made from 8020, with polycarbonate panels cut to fit in the sides. 3D-printed clamps hold each panel in place and squeeze themselves tight when the sheet is inserted. A plastic cover is velcroed onto the front and sides to give access to the spray gantry.

To ventilate the toxic fumes, I printed custom brackets that hold an exhaust fan in place and connected it to foil ducting that carries the fumes out through the window. To protect the board from the spray, I made another custom bracket that mounts the board and solenoid driver outside the enclosure.
